Free mental health help in Nigeria.

Helplines, peer circles, public hospitals and the places people forget to check — what is genuinely free, what is only cheap, and how to tell.

Last reviewed 1 October 2026 by Be Empowered Network. This page is information, not medical advice.

Free help exists in Nigeria. It is just badly signposted.

If money is the reason you have not asked for help yet, you are not unusual and you are not stuck. Nigeria has very few mental health professionals for its population — roughly 300 psychiatrists for more than 200 million people, by the most commonly cited estimate — and most people who need care never reach it. Cost is one of the biggest reasons why.

But "free" does exist here. It just is not advertised. This page lists what is genuinely free in Nigeria, what is low-cost rather than free, and how to tell the difference before you spend money you do not have.

Option one

Helplines — free, immediate, no appointment

The fastest free support in Nigeria is a phone line. You do not need a referral, an account or a diagnosis.

  • MANI (Mentally Aware Nigeria Initiative) — 0809 111 6264. A peer-led line for anyone struggling, widely used by young Nigerians.
  • Nigeria Suicide Prevention Initiative — 0806 210 6493. Counselling and suicide prevention.
  • She Writes Woman — a women-led mental health organisation running a support line and safe-space groups.
  • 112 — the national emergency number, for when someone's life is at risk.

Lines get busy and hours change. If one does not answer, try the next rather than giving up. Our mental health directory keeps the current list.

Option two

Peer support circles — free, and you can just listen

A peer support circle is a small, confidential group of people who have been through something similar, held by trained facilitators. Be Empowered Network runs them free, online and in person, and you do not need a diagnosis or a referral to come. You are allowed to attend and say nothing at all.

A circle is not therapy and our facilitators are not clinicians. What it gives you is the thing most people are actually missing: somewhere to say the true version out loud without being judged, prayed at or told to be grateful. For a lot of people that is enough. For others it is the step that makes professional care feel possible.

Option three

Public hospitals — low-cost, not free

Federal neuropsychiatric hospitals and the psychiatry departments of teaching hospitals are the most affordable route to a psychiatrist in Nigeria. They are subsidised, not free: expect to pay for registration, consultation and medication, and expect to wait.

They are also where to go for anything that needs medical treatment rather than conversation — psychosis, severe depression, bipolar disorder, or a reaction to medication. Our directory lists them by state, including Yaba and LUTH in Lagos, Aro in Ogun, and the federal hospitals in Enugu, Kaduna, Maiduguri, Calabar, Benin City and Sokoto.

If you are in crisis, you do not need an appointment. Go to the nearest hospital or call 112.

Option four

Places people forget to check

  • Your university or polytechnic. Most Nigerian universities have a counselling unit that is free to enrolled students. It is usually under-used because nobody mentions it.
  • Your employer. Larger employers and multinationals increasingly offer an employee assistance programme with a set number of free confidential sessions. Ask HR what the policy is — they are not told who used it.
  • NGOs and foundations. Several Nigerian organisations offer free or subsidised counselling, including Asido Foundation in Ibadan and She Writes Woman.
  • Your religious community, carefully. A supportive pastor or imam can be a real help alongside treatment. Be wary of anyone who tells you that medication shows a lack of faith, or that what you have is only spiritual.

If you can pay a little

What it costs if you do end up paying

If you are considering paying privately, know the real range before you call. From what people are quoted when we help them look, private therapy in Nigeria commonly runs from about ₦40,000 a session to ₦300,000 and beyond, depending on who you see and how complex the need is.

We would rather be blunt about that than encouraging: for most Nigerians those fees put a sustained course of private therapy out of reach, and a course of treatment is never one session. That is why this page exists. The free and subsidised options above are not a lesser substitute for people who cannot afford the real thing — for most of the country they are the route to support.

If you do pay, always ask the fee before the first session and ask whether there is a sliding scale; some therapists keep a few reduced-fee places they do not advertise. And treat the low figures in older online articles with caution — they have not kept pace with what clinicians now charge.

Common questions

Questions people ask.

Is there completely free therapy in Nigeria?

Free one-to-one therapy with a licensed clinician is rare. What is genuinely free and widely available is helpline support, peer support circles, student counselling units and some NGO services. Public psychiatric hospitals are subsidised rather than free. If you need a clinician and cannot pay, ask about a sliding scale and ask us — we will help you look.

Do I need a diagnosis or a referral to get help?

Not for a helpline, a peer support circle or an NGO service. You can walk into a public psychiatric hospital without a referral too, though you will usually pay a registration and consultation fee.

I am not in Lagos. Does any of this apply to me?

Yes. The helplines are nationwide and our peer support circles run online, so you can join from anywhere in Nigeria. The directory lists hospitals across Lagos, Ogun, Oyo, Enugu, Edo, Kaduna, Borno, Cross River and Sokoto.

Will anyone find out that I asked for help?

Helplines and circles are confidential. What is shared in a circle stays in the circle. The only limit is safety: if someone is at immediate risk of harm, we will help them reach emergency support.

Is it still worth talking to someone if I cannot afford treatment?

Yes. Being heard by someone who understands is not a consolation prize — it is the thing most people in Nigeria never get. It also makes it far easier to take the next step when treatment does become possible.

You do not have to work this out alone.
